# Narration for the demo video. One block per scene; the id matches a renderer
# in scripts/video_scenes.py. make_video.py records each clip and times that
# scene's animation to the measured length.
#
# Voice: Kokoro af_heart at speed 1.04 -- roughly 165 wpm, just above the
# 145-165 engagement dip Guo et al. measured across 862 edX videos.
#
# Budget: readable characters on screen, not spoken words, is the binding
# limit -- about 20 per second. Say "the Nano", never "the box".

@ meta | pre-roll joke, deliberately short
Quick thing: we made this whole video on the Nano. The voice, the music, every
frame.

@ what | THE ONE-LINER, before anything else
NeoFold Edge takes a tumour's DNA and picks the targets a cancer vaccine should
aim at. It runs on one small computer, completely offline.

@ hook | Rosie, then the 75% counting up
Here's why that matters. In twenty twenty-four, a dog named Rosie was dying of
cancer. Her owner isn't a biologist. He's an engineer. He had her tumour
sequenced, used AI to pick the targets, and designed her a vaccine. Her biggest
tumour shrank by three quarters.

@ question | eight cloud steps stacking up against one
So why can't every hospital do that? The input is a human genome. It identifies
the patient, and their family. In most places it can't legally leave the
building. Sending it out costs weeks.

@ problem | 1,890 dots dimming to the fifty that matter
And the hard part was never making the vaccine. It's choosing what goes in it.
One tumour gives about nineteen hundred candidates. Maybe fifty are real. In the
biggest trial ever run, six percent worked.

@ funnel | the funnel, actually funnelling
So here's the funnel. Nineteen hundred candidates, screened in nine seconds on
the CPU. Anything matching a healthy human protein gets cut. Twenty-two survive.
The top five get folded in three dimensions.

@ dashboard | THE REAL INTERFACE, pushing in from wide
This is the actual interface, running on the Nano. Every candidate the screen
considered, ranked, with the reason it made the cut written next to it in plain
English. The whole funnel is on the left. Nothing here is a mock-up.

@ wow | the flagged row, surround dimmed away
Watch this row. It's from KIT, a real cancer driver, and by every conventional
measure it ranked first. It's also an exact match to a healthy human protein.
Target that, and you attack the patient.

@ structure | the 3D complex and the pre-registered contact
For the finalists we predict the actual three-dimensional structure. Here's the
tumour peptide sitting in the patient's HLA molecule. We measure one contact,
chosen in advance from a crystal structure: two and a half angstroms, against
the crystal's two point seven.

@ evidence | ROC curves and the enrichment bars, including the failure
Does the screen work? We tested it against two and a half thousand peptides that
were physically tested on human T-cells. Area under the curve, point seven
eight. Five to six times better than chance. And we left our own failures on
screen: this filter scored worse than random.

@ holdout | confidence against measured error
Here's the finding we're proudest of. Nine crystal structures the model had
never seen. Confidence barely moves. Real error varies by a factor of two. The
model has no idea when it's wrong, so we never rank on it.

@ summary | the local LLM panel with its guardrails
Then a language model on the same machine writes it up. It only gets the facts
on the left, and every number it prints is checked against them. If it invents
one, the summary is rejected.

@ nano | four model chips, memory bar, watts
All of this on one HP ZGX Nano. Four models resident at once. A hundred and
twenty-eight gigabytes of unified memory. Thirty-eight watts, about what a
laptop pulls.

@ proof | the thirteen-minute bar and the offline badge
One tumour, start to finish, about thirteen minutes. No internet connection at
any point.

@ close | tagline and repo
A cancer research lab that fits on a desk. And never phones home.

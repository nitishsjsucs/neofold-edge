# Narration. One block per scene; the id matches a renderer in
# scripts/video_scenes.py. make_video.py records each clip and times that
# scene's animation to the measured length.
#
# THE PRODUCT SCENES ARE WRITTEN AS A GUIDED TOUR. Each one names its targets
# in the order the pointers appear, so the picture and the voice stay in step.
# The pointer targets are the measured regions in video/app-regions.json --
# "on the left, the funnel" lands a marker on the funnel, not near it.
#
# Voice: Kokoro af_heart, speed 1.04 (~165 wpm). Say "the Nano", never "the box".

@ meta | pre-roll joke
Quick thing: we made this whole video on the Nano. The voice, the music, every
frame.

@ what | THE ONE-LINER
NeoFold Edge takes a tumour's DNA and picks the targets a cancer vaccine should
aim at. It runs on one small computer, completely offline.

@ hook | Rosie, then the 75% counting up
Here's why that matters. In twenty twenty-four, a dog named Rosie was dying of
cancer. Her owner isn't a biologist. He's an engineer. He had her tumour
sequenced, used AI to pick the targets, and designed her a vaccine. Her biggest
tumour shrank by three quarters.

@ question | eight cloud steps against one
So why can't every hospital do that? The input is a human genome. It identifies
the patient, and their family. In most places it can't legally leave the
building. Sending it out costs weeks.

@ problem | 1,890 dots dimming to fifty
And the hard part was never making the vaccine. It's choosing what goes in it.
One tumour gives about nineteen hundred candidates. Maybe fifty are real. In the
biggest trial ever run, six percent worked.

@ dashboard | POINTERS: funnel -> table -> detail card
This is the interface, running on the Nano. On the left, the funnel: nineteen
hundred candidates screened in nine seconds, down to twenty-two. Below it, every
one of them ranked, with the reason it made the cut written in plain English.
And on the right, whichever candidate you select, with its evidence.

@ wow | POINTER: the flagged row
Now look at this row. KIT D816V is a real cancer driver, and it carries the
strongest mutant-versus-normal signal in the entire run. It is also an exact
match to a healthy human protein. Target it, and you attack the patient.

@ structure | POINTERS: viewer -> contact panel
For the finalists, we predict the structure. That's the tumour peptide sitting
in the patient's HLA molecule. And this contact was chosen in advance, from a
published crystal. We predict two and a half angstroms. The crystal says two
point seven.

@ evidence | POINTERS: stat row -> ROC -> enrichment bars
Does the screen actually work? Two and a half thousand peptides, every one of
them physically tested on human T-cells. Area under the curve, point seven
eight. And we left our own failure on screen: this filter scored worse than
random, so we stopped using it.

@ holdout | POINTERS: the flat cloud -> the correlation
Here's the finding we're proudest of. Nine crystal structures the model had
never seen. Confidence barely moves. Real error varies by a factor of two.
Correlation, minus zero point two three. The model has no idea when it's wrong,
so we never rank on it.

@ summary | POINTERS: the facts -> the verified line
Then a language model on the same machine writes it up. It only gets these
facts. Every number it prints is checked against them, and if it invents one,
the summary is thrown away.

@ nano | four model chips, memory bar, watts
All of this on one HP ZGX Nano. Four models resident at once. A hundred and
twenty-eight gigabytes of unified memory. Thirty-eight watts, about what a
laptop pulls.

@ proof | the thirteen-minute bar
One tumour, start to finish, about thirteen minutes. No internet connection at
any point.

@ close | tagline and repo
A cancer research lab that fits on a desk. And never phones home.
